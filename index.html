Questions Everyone Keeps Asking

How long does a personal injury case in Kent actually take?

Most straightforward car accident cases with clear liability resolve in six to twelve months. Cases that involve significant injuries, disputed liability, or insurance companies that refuse to negotiate reasonably can stretch to two years or longer if they go to trial. Don't let anyone promise you a fast resolution on a complicated case — that's usually a sign they're planning to settle fast and cheap, not fight hard for maximum value.

What does a Kent personal injury attorney cost upfront?

Nothing, at least at the firms worth hiring. Every legitimate personal injury attorney in Washington works on contingency — meaning they take a percentage of your recovery (typically 33% pre-litigation, sometimes 40% if the case goes to trial) and collect nothing if you don't win. If anyone asks you for money upfront on a personal injury case, walk away immediately.

Should I accept the first settlement offer from the insurance company?

Almost certainly not. First offers from insurance adjusters are designed to close your case before you fully understand the extent of your injuries and damages. A herniated disc, for example, may not produce its full symptoms until weeks after an accident — and once you settle, you cannot go back for more money. Talk to an attorney before you sign anything, even if the offer sounds reasonable.

What if I was partially at fault for my accident?

Washington is a pure comparative negligence state, which means you can recover damages even if you were partly at fault — your recovery is simply reduced by your percentage of fault. If you were 20% at fault and your damages are $100,000, you can still recover $80,000. Insurance companies will often try to inflate your percentage of fault to reduce their exposure. An experienced attorney knows how to push back on those tactics.

Do I need a police report to file a personal injury claim?

A police report is helpful but not strictly required. It can provide an independent account of what happened and who was cited, which is valuable evidence. If police weren't called to your accident, you should still document everything you can: photos, witness contact information, a written account of events created as soon as possible after the accident. The more contemporary your documentation, the better.

How do I know if my case is worth pursuing?

A genuine personal injury case typically involves documented medical treatment, verifiable lost wages, and an at-fault party with insurance coverage or assets. Cases with very minor injuries and no significant financial impact are harder to pursue profitably on contingency, and a good attorney will tell you that honestly. If your injuries are real, your bills are real, and the other party was at fault, your case almost certainly warrants at least a consultation.

What's the statute of limitations for personal injury cases in Washington?

Three years from the date of injury for most personal injury cases in Washington State. That sounds like a long time, but building a strong case takes time, and evidence gets harder to preserve as months pass. Witnesses move, surveillance footage gets overwritten, and physical evidence disappears. Don't wait until month 35 to call an attorney.

Will my case go to trial?

The vast majority of personal injury cases — somewhere in the range of 95% — settle before trial. But the willingness to go to trial matters enormously anyway. Insurance companies track which attorneys actually litigate and which ones always settle. Firms with a genuine reputation for taking cases to trial consistently get better settlement offers, because the insurer knows the threat of trial is real. Ask any attorney you're considering about their trial experience before you sign anything.

What should I bring to my first consultation with a personal injury attorney?

Anything you have: the police report if one was filed, photos from the scene, medical records and bills you've received so far, any communications from the other driver's insurance company (especially any forms they've asked you to sign), and your own insurance information. The more context you can provide, the more useful the consultation will be. And don't sign anything the insurance company sends you before that meeting.

Can I switch attorneys mid-case if I'm unhappy?

Yes, and it happens more often than the legal industry would like to admit. You have the right to change representation at any point. There will likely be fee-sharing considerations between your outgoing and incoming attorney, but that gets worked out between the firms — it should not be your primary concern if your current representation isn't serving you well. A good attorney will never hold your case hostage over the prospect of a fee dispute.
